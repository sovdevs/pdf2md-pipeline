Spinal Anaesthesia and/or Epidural Anaesthesia

For epidural anaesthesia, it is injected into the space outside the dura mater (epidural space) either
- in the lumbar spine (lumbar epidural anaesthesia)
- the thoracic spine (thoracic epidural anaesthesia)

As a rule, the injection is not very painful because the injection site is numbed with a local anaesthetic agent beforehand.

In spinal anaesthesia, the anaesthesia takes effect within a few minutes, in epidural anaesthesia, it takes effect within 15-30 minutes in most cases. In spinal anaesthesia and lumbar epidural anaesthesia, the lower pelvic region and legs first become warm and then heavy and numb: the patient is unable to move his/her legs at all or may move them only to a limited extent for several hours. In thoracic epidural anaesthesia, ability to move the legs is retained at least to a limited extent. Pain medications (e.g. opioids) take effect quickly and the effects last longer than those of the local anaesthetic agents. Mobility of the legs is not restricted by pain medications.

Spinal anaesthesia and lumbar epidural anaesthesia are appropriate in particular for operations on the abdomen, the perineum, the pelvis, the groin and the legs.

Thoracic epidural anaesthesia, which is combined with general anaesthesia in most cases, is appropriate for procedures on the upper abdomen and chest. If general anaesthesia is proposed in your case, you will receive separate counselling on it and the associated procedures and risks.

Need for a change to general anaesthesia

It may become necessary to change from spinal/epidural anaesthesia to general anaesthesia if regional anaesthesia does not have an adequate effect or if the effects wear off too soon and repeated injections or additional nerve block are not possible or if regional anaesthesia spreads to an extensive area or other complications (e.g. cardiocirculatory problems) occur.

General anaesthesia results in a loss of consciousness and pain perception. The patient falls into a sleep-like state. A distinction is made between the following procedures:

1. Intravenous anaesthesia:
   A fast-acting anaesthetic (narcotic) agent is injected into a vein via a cannula or a thin tube (catheter). For longer procedures, it is administered repeatedly or continuously (intravenous general anaesthesia), or intravenous general anaesthesia is combined with one of the following procedures (combined general anaesthesia).
2. Mask anaesthesia:
   Gaseous anaesthetic agents and oxygen are administered through a breathing mask which fits tightly over the mouth and nose.
3. Anaesthesia using a laryngeal mask or endotracheal anaesthesia:

After intravenous induction of the anaesthesia. i.e. after you have fallen asleep,
- for general anaesthesia with a laryngeal mask. the doctor inserts a breathing tube through the mouth and advances it until it is covering the laryngeal inlet: s/he subsequently secures the airway by inflating the cuff.
- for endotracheal anaesthesia, s/he inserts a breathing tube via the mouth or nose into the windpipe. An inflatable cuff near the tip of the tube seals the trachea (windpipe).

Both procedures keep the airway open for administration of oxygen and gaseous anaesthetic agents and facilitate artificial ventilation. In particular, endotracheal anaesthesia reduces the risk of aspiration of saliva and stomach contents into the lungs. For endotracheal anaesthesia, medications (muscle relaxants) are administered in almost all cases. One reason for this is that it facilitates gentle insertion of the breathing tube. These medications are administered only in individual cases for laryngeal mask anaesthesia.

In both procedures, a catheter may be placed for repeated administration of the anaesthetic agent or for continuous administration of anaesthetic agents and/or pain medications. If required, correct location of the catheter can be verified by ultrasound or also X-ray. After the operation, the catheter can be used for management of pain and may be connected to a medication pump if necessary. You will be counselled about the pain management in a separate patient-doctor discussion.

Risks and possible associated complications

The frequency rates stated are not the same as those stated in the package inserts of medications. They are only a general estimate and are intended for weighing the risks against each other. Despite the greatest care taken in the planning and performance of the anaesthesia and in monitoring of the vital bodily functions, complications can arise which can even become life-threatening under certain circumstances and necessitate additional treatment or further surgery. Pre-existing/Underlying disease and individual unusual circumstances can influence the rate of complications.

General risks and possible associated complications

The following complications can be caused by injections and placement of indwelling and other catheters, e.g. also for pain management:

- Nerve injury and minor nerve damage can occur and can cause symptoms such as disorders of sensation, sensitivity to touch, numbness, disorders of movement and pain, which are temporary in most cases but in an unfavourable case, they can also be permanent.
- Large haematomas and injury to blood vessels can occur, and in rare cases, these can cause severe bleeding.
- Infection, e.g. at the injection site or along the course of the needle track or the catheter, is rare. Possible consequences are e.g. an injection track abscess, necrosis (death) of tissue, scarring or irritation/inflammation of the vein. In extremely rare cases, infection causes life-threatening blood poisoning (sepsis).
- Chronic pain or permanent paralysis can occur after severe nerve injuries, haematomas or inflammation. However, these are also extremely rare.
- Skin, tissue and nerve damage, with paralysis of the arms/legs in the most extreme case, as a result of pressure, strain or overextension/stretching while positioning the patient on the examination/treatment/operating table during regional or general anaesthesia (damage due to the positioning of the patient) cannot be excluded with certainty; in most cases, the damage will disappear within a few months; however, in very rare cases, it may become permanent.

Specific risks and possible associated complications in the event of a change to general anaesthesia
- The incidence of nausea and vomiting has been lessened. Life-threatening incidents due to aspiration of saliva or stomach contents into the lungs occur very rarely: this complication necessitates intensive care monitoring/treatment.
- In rare cases, a spasmodic closure of the airway (laryngospasm/bronchospasm) occurs; this can, however, be managed with medications.
- Intubation/Use of a laryngeal mask can cause temporary difficulty swallowing and hoarseness. Very rarely, injury to the pharynx, jaw, larynx and windpipe, as weil as damage to the vocal chords with permanent dysphonia (hoarseness) and shortness of breath may occur. In rare cases, temporary disorders of sensation of the tongue can occur which can be permanent in even rarer cases. Damage, in particular to loose or carious teeth. to implants and fixed dentures (e.g. crowns. bridges, prosthesis). as weil as loss ofteeth can occur.
- In rare cases. patients can regain consciousness (intraoperative awareness) during general anaesthesia despite careful monitoring, and in even rarer cases. the patient is able to feel pain ( pain awareness).

Specific risks and possible complications of the spinal and epidural anaesthesia
- lf the anaesthetic agent spreads too extensively during injection (e.g. total spinal anaesthesia) or if it gains ingress into the blood stream, this could elicit seizures and lead to unconsciousness and severe cardiocirculatory reaction which can also be life-threatening in very rare cases and necessitate artificial respiration and treatment on an intensive care unit.
- After placing a regional catheter for pain management, infection at the puncture site can occur. in particular if treatment is prolonged; this complication would necessitate treatment with antibiotics and in an occasional case even an operation.
- A loop can occur in the catheter. making removal more difficult and causing injury to vessels and nerves; in very rare cases, this can also lead to breakage of the catheter. Under certain circurnstances, this can necessitate an operation to remove it.
- Ternporary urinary retention occurs frequently after spinal/epidural anaesthesia; this can necessitate placement of a bladder catheter for a short period.
- During thoracic epidural anaesthesia, air can gain ingress into the pleural cavity (pneumothorax): this is manifested as laboured breathing as weil as pain in the ehest. lt may be necessaryto remove the air by suction pump.
- After spinal anaesthesia but also after epidural anaesthesia in which the dura mater is punctured (unintentionally), severe headache can occur. This may necessitate specific treatment (e.g. injection of the patient's own blood into the epidural space, so-called "blood patch"), As a rule, the headaches go away after a few days. However, they can also persist for a prolonged time (in exceptional cases even several months or years). Back pain occurs frequently but goesaway within a few days as a rule. Chronic back pain is extremely rare.
- Hypersensitivity/incompatibility reaction or mild allergic reacilon le.g. to anaesrheric ageuis, pain medications, other medications, sterilising agents, latex) is rare. This can manifest e.g. as nausea,itching and a skin rash. Breathing difficultyor reactions affecting the circulation (e.g. drop in blood pressure. slowing of the heartbeat) can be treated quickly in most cases.
- Severe allergic reaction and life-threatening cornplications of other origin (e.g. cardiocirculatory, respiratory and organ failure, formation of blood clots that are carried through the blood stream and vessel blockage) with severe permanent consequences under certain circumstances (e.g. brain damage, damage to other organs,paralysis, pulmonary embolism) are very rare.
- In extremely rare cases, as a result of a massive, life-threatening metabolic derailment, an extreme increase in body temperature (malignant hyperthermia) occurs in patients with a specific genetic predisposition. Immediate treatment with medications on an intensive care unit would then be necessary.
- Separation from their usual surroundings and stress caused by the operation and anaesthesia and other underlying disease can lead to confusion, particularly in elderly patients, although this is usually of a temporary nature.
- After spinal anaesthesia,life-threatening brain haemorrhage and accumulation of blood or fluid under the dura mater (the membrane surrounding the brain) (subdural haematoma/hygroma) and also permanent worsening of hearing and vision and disorders of potency can occur in very rare cases. The same applies for epidural anaesthesia in which the dura mater is punctured (unintentionally).
- A direct injury to the spinal cord during spinal as well as lumbar epidural anaesthesia can be virtuallyexclu-ded, because as a rule, the spinal cord ends above the level of the injection site (see fig.); this type of injury is very rare in thoracic epidural anaesthesia. Permanent paralysis (e.g, impairment of intestine/bladder ernpty-ing), in the most extreme case paraplegia, due to haematomas, inflammation/infection, nerve or spinal cord injuries or caused by the injected agents, occur in extremely rare cases. The same applies for occurrence of meningitis.

Additional and subsequent procedures that may be necessary
- Measures that may be necessary in preparation for, during and after the procedure (e.g. to monitor and maintain vital body functions. such as placement of a central venous catheter, administration of medications or essential X-ray examinations) are also not without risk.
- The risk of infection (e.g. with hepatitis, AIDS) after transfusion of foreign donor blood has become extremely rare. After a transfusion, it is possible to perform follow-up laboratory evaluation. lf it is expected that you will need to receive transfusion of foreign donor blood or blood components or measures to avoid transfusion of foreign donor blood (e.g. donation of own blood, blood thinning, recovering and processing of shed blood and re-infusion of the recovered red blood cells [mechanical autotransfusion)) are possible, you will receive counselling about these procedures and their associatedrisks in a separate discussion
